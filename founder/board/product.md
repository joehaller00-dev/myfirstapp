# Product lens
Score: 3/10 | Vote: PASS

The lens asks what the one thing is, and what the customer's first five minutes feel like. Right now Nora Furnish can't answer either. It is a catalog, not a product: 1,819 active items, 185 collections and about 100 product types, several of them duplicated ("Pendants"/"Pendant Lighting"). It sells Halloween inflatables and shower curtains next to $1,000+ chandeliers, offers custom neon, custom curtains and custom chandeliers, and has 8 industry pages for a trade channel with no trade revenue. The pitch, "modern lighting and home decor with free US shipping and 30 day returns", describes a policy, not a product. Any dropship store could say the same sentence.

## What is strong
- **A real signal is buried in the data.** Google organic converts at about 0.37%, against about 0.1% overall, and the orders getting bigger are lighting: Cloud Pendant x4 at $1,170, Motion Sensor Stair Light at $862, Vanity Picture Light at $285. Someone searching for a specific light, landing on it and buying is the only experience that works today.
- **The margin pays for craft.** Recorded unit costs are 5x to 17x below retail. Few businesses can afford to inspect every unit, ship faster and take returns for free. This one could, if it chose to.
- **Lighting suits this lens.** A statement pendant is seen every day and judged on detail. If the product and the delivery were excellent, customers would show it off to guests.
- **There is groundwork to build on:** 31 blog posts, a custom theme, and a store that has been live for eight months.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **The price promises more than the product delivers.** The positioning is "high-ticket statement lighting". The sampled products cost $8 to $14 and sell for $124 to $202. When a customer pays $124 and opens a box holding an $8 light, sent from an unnamed supplier with no visible QC, the brand loses them. Craft in the parts nobody sees is exactly what dropshipping can't promise. Four of 14 real orders were refunded (29%). That sample is too small to prove anything, but it is the first number to watch, and the brief records no refund reasons.
2. **The business owns almost none of the experience.** After checkout, every touchpoint belongs to someone else. "In stock" items carry zero inventory and take 10-12 business days. Made-to-order takes 3-4 weeks plus 9-10 days in transit. Packaging, condition on arrival and tracking all sit with the supplier, and a buyer who changes their mind about a chandelier pays to ship it back. The weak points are the handoffs: store to supplier, supplier to carrier, customer back to store. Nothing in the brief shows anyone owns them.
3. **There is no focus, so there is no product.** The 1,819 SKUs, the 8 live discount mechanisms (WELCOME10, COMEBACK10, a 20% membership that also applies to sale items, "Kevin 15" and others) and the six service lines all pull attention away from the one experience that converts. The homepage drew 3,275 sessions and 0 orders, and visitors who do arrive from search mostly land on cheap items (rope lights, a shower curtain, solar flood lights). A store that tries to be everything shows visitors no reason to trust it with a $600 light. Constant discounting also tells them the list price isn't real.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Say what Nora Furnish is in one sentence, without mentioning shipping, returns or discounts.
- Have you held each of your top 20 lights in your own hands? What did the box, the finish and the instructions feel like?
- Why was each of the 4 orders refunded? Did the $862 stair-light customer return it and then buy it again at $819 because of the product or because of the price?
- If you could keep only 50 products, which would they be, and why those?
- Can you name the supplier behind every live product? What happens today when one arrives broken?
- Open questions I can't answer from the brief:
  - Are these same items sold elsewhere for much less, so that a customer could find the $8 light at $20? **/founder-competitors**
  - Will the target buyer wait 2-5 weeks for a $300-$1,000 light from a brand they don't know? **/founder-consumer**
  - What does one order really cost once supplier shipping, refunds, discounts and processing fees are counted? **/founder-cfo**

## If FUND IF: the conditions
Not applicable, because the vote is PASS. The current business is a generic dropship catalog with no owned experience, and making the store "perfect in every way" would only polish that catalog. These are the steps that would bring it back to this board:
1. Cut to one category the founder can stand behind. The sales data points to statement pendants and wall lights. Aim for about 50-100 SKUs, each sampled in hand.
2. Choose products whose quality matches the price, or lower the price to match the quality. Drop any item the founder wouldn't hang in their own home.
3. Take back the handoffs: an inspected or stocked set of best-sellers, honest delivery dates instead of "in stock" labels that hide a two-week wait, branded packaging, and free returns on lighting.
4. Replace the discount stack with one clear price.
5. Show a refund rate under about 10% across at least 30 real orders in the focused category.

None of these steps needs to be clever, but each one is a decision to say no.
