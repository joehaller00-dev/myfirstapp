# Offers lens
Score: 3/10 | Vote: PASS

## What is strong
- **The margins leave room to build a strong offer.** The sampled unit costs are 5x to 17x below retail (a $8.66 ceiling light sells for $123.99). Shipping and fees will take some of that, but there is clearly room to pay for a real guarantee, faster shipping or bonuses. Most stores that compete on price have no such room.
- **The buyers have money.** People renovating, interior designers, short-term-rental owners and hospitality buyers spend $200 to $1,000+ on fixtures. Average order value rose from $56 in August to $995 in October, and the Cloud Pendant sold as a set of 4 for $1,170. People do buy here at high prices.
- **One channel works.** Google organic converts at about 0.37% (7 orders from 1,889 sessions). Buyers who arrive searching for something specific do sometimes buy. That is a signal of intent, though not yet proof of an offer.
- **The trade pages point at the market where the pain is.** Designers, contractors and short-term-rental owners have real, costly problems: deadlines, sourcing many matching fixtures, specs, ordering in bulk. Homeowners mostly *want* a nicer room. Trade buyers *need* a project to finish on time.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **Buyers have no reason to believe it will work, so likelihood is near zero.** This is an unknown brand selling $300 to $1,000+ fixtures it has never handled, from suppliers whose names and quality checks are unknown. There are no reviews or proof the buyer can see. The data matches: about 0.1% of sessions buy, and 4 of 14 real orders (29%) were refunded. In this lens, perceived likelihood is where most offers die, and this one is failing there now. More traffic would mostly bring more refunds.
2. **It is a commodity whose only lever is discounts.** There are 1,819 products across lighting, Halloween inflatables, shower curtains and fragrance, with about 100 messy product types. That reads as a general store, not a destination. The reasons it gives to buy are all price cuts: WELCOME10, COMEBACK10, 10% off 2 items, 15% off 3+, 20% for members, "Kevin 15", and 165 items marked price-reduced against compare-at prices. If the same products are sold elsewhere for less, the margin that is this store's best asset will be competed away. Whether the same products are sold elsewhere is an open question. **/founder-competitors** answers it.
3. **Delivery time and buyer effort are the worst parts of the value equation.** In-stock items take 10 to 12 business days. Made-to-order items take 3 to 4 weeks of production plus 9 to 10 business days in transit. A renovator on a schedule pays for that wait. If the buyer changes their mind, they pay return shipping on a bulky, fragile fixture, and it must be unused and in its original packaging. So the store asks for more patience and more risk than a buyer expects at these prices, and offers no guarantee to make up for it. The returns terms protect the store, not the buyer.

## Market check (four tests)
- **The pain is massive:** weak for homeowners (a nice-to-have); moderate to strong for trade buyers working to a deadline.
- **Buyers have money:** yes.
- **Buyers are easy to find and reach:** partly. Google organic works at small volume. Customer acquisition cost is unknown because no meaningful paid ads have run. **/founder-cfo** needs this number, and so does a small paid test.
- **The market is growing:** unknown. I will not guess. **/founder-competitors** can size it.

## The questions I would ask the founder
1. Who is the one buyer? Pick one: homeowner, interior designer or short-term-rental owner. Each needs a different offer.
2. Why were the 4 orders refunded: quality, delivery time, or the product not matching its photos? Your refunds are the only real customer feedback you have.
3. What is the landed cost of one order, including the supplier's shipping, payment fees and the cost of a return? Without that, nobody knows if a guarantee is affordable. (**/founder-cfo**)
4. Can buyers find the same products elsewhere, and at what price? (**/founder-competitors**)
5. Would a target buyer pay full price with no discount code, and what would make them say yes? (**/founder-consumer**)
6. What can you guarantee and actually deliver: a delivery date, free returns, a quality check before shipping? Do your suppliers support it?
7. Could you double the price for one segment and still be the obvious choice? If not, what is missing?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
Not a FUND IF. As it stands, this is a general catalogue store with slow shipping, no proof and only discounts to offer, and the traction so far (14 orders, 29% refunded) does not argue otherwise. I would vote again if the founder came back with:
- **One segment and one category.** For example, lighting for short-term-rental owners or designers, with the catalogue cut to the products that serve them.
- **An offer stack built around that buyer's problems.** For example: a guaranteed ship-by date, help choosing fixtures, matching sets, free returns on the first order and trade pricing. Each item costed by /founder-cfo so the guarantee is affordable.
- **Proof.** Reviews, photos of installed fixtures, or named project work from the first trade customers.
- **Fewer, honest discounts.** Drop the stacked codes and replace them with value the buyer can see.
- **Evidence it converts.** A small test, run with /founder-consumer before launch and then with real traffic, showing buyers in that segment purchasing at full price with a refund rate well below today's.
